Where the data comes from

Everything is a model forecast for one point: Mid-Collins at Alta, 9,662 ft. The exceptions are the snowfall chart, which also shows three other points at Alta, the temperature chart, which adds the Base and Top of Collins, and the second pair of wind roses, which are for the top of Mt Baldy. Apart from the Weather Stations table, nothing here is a measurement, including the "Now" tiles.

  • Main forecast: the US GFS model from Open-Meteo, with the high-resolution HRRR model filling in the first one to two days. It runs 16 days ahead in hourly steps.
  • Comparison models: the NBM (the National Weather Service's blend of many models, about 11 days) and the NAM (about 2.5 days). The ECMWF AI model appears on the cloud chart only.
  • National Weather Service: the text forecast and the "NWS snow" column in the table.

The four snow tiles are buttons. Click one to make every chart show only that period: 24 hours, 72 hours, 7 days or 16 days.

Weather Stations

The only measured data on the page. It shows the latest reading from each station that is reporting, taken from the National Weather Service's public data feed: temperature, wind, gust, and the direction the wind is coming from. A reading more than three hours old is marked "old".

  • Stations: Alta Base, Alta Guard House, Alta Collins, Top of Collins, Hidden Peak at Snowbird, and Mt Baldy. Any that are not sending data are listed under the table and appear on their own once they report.
  • Model check: the line under the table compares the wind a summit station measured over the last few days with the wind the model gave for the Mt Baldy point over the same hours. It uses Mt Baldy's own station when it reports, otherwise Hidden Peak, a summit about a kilometre away. The "typical miss" is the average size of the hourly difference.

Click a station in the table to see its recent history below: a wind chart (speed, gusts and direction arrows), a wind rose built from the measured wind, a temperature chart, and then every reading line by line, newest first. The history covers the station's last 300 readings, which is roughly two to five days depending on how often it reports.

The stations do not feed into any forecast chart or calculation. They are there so you can compare the forecast with what is happening on the mountain.

How snow is calculated

Models forecast precipitation as liquid water. The page turns that into inches of snow in three steps, hour by hour.

1. Snow water (SWE). The part of the precipitation that falls as snow, still measured as liquid:

snow water = total precipitation − rain − showers

2. Snow ratio (Kuchera method). How many inches of snow one inch of water makes. Colder air makes lighter snow and a higher ratio. The method takes the warmest temperature anywhere between the ground and about 18,000 ft (the surface plus five levels above it), in kelvin:

if warmest temperature is above 271.16 K (28.4°F): ratio = 12 + 2 × (271.16 − warmest)
otherwise: ratio = 12 + (271.16 − warmest)

The ratio is kept between 3 and 30. Examples: a warmest temperature of 30°F gives about 10 to 1, and 20°F gives about 17 to 1.

3. Snow and density.

snow (inches) = snow water × ratio
density (% water) = 100 ÷ ratio

So a 10 to 1 ratio is 10% density (average snow) and a 17 to 1 ratio is about 6% (light powder).

Snow tiles

The "Next 24 h", "Next 72 h", "7-day" and "16-day" tiles show the snow and snow water added up over that period, using the average of the snowfall models at Mid-Collins (see the next chart). The "Now", "Wind" and "Clouds" tiles are the GFS model's current conditions.

Snow Summary

A day-by-day view of snow at Mid-Collins: the last five days, today, and the next fourteen days. The number above each bar is that day's total in inches. Above the bars, each storm has a bracket spanning its days, with the storm's total snow written over it.

  • Blue bars (forecast): the average of the snowfall models, the same figure as the snow tiles. Light blue is snow falling while the sun is up and dark blue is snow falling at night, both within that calendar day.
  • Grey bars (already fallen): the GFS model's estimate of what fell, run through the same snow calculation. It is not a measurement, because none of the weather stations the page can reach report snowfall. Today's bar has a grey part for the hours already gone and a blue part for the rest of the day.
  • Storm totals: a storm is a run of back-to-back days with at least 0.1" of snow each. One day with less than that ends the storm, so two bursts of snow separated by a dry day count as two storms. The total adds up everything in those bars, including the grey "already fallen" part if the storm has already started.

This chart always shows the full range; the 24 h, 72 h, 7-day and 16-day tiles do not zoom it.

Snowfall by model

Running totals of snow from now at four points on the mountain, one colour each: Base (8,560 ft), Mid-Collins (9,662 ft), Top of Collins (10,443 ft) and Mt Baldy (11,068 ft). For each point the thin lines are the individual models (GFS solid, NBM dashed, NAM dotted) and the thick line is their average. Where a colour's thin lines sit close together the models agree; where they fan out the forecast is uncertain. Click a location in the legend to hide or show it.

Each point is run through the same snow calculation with its own temperatures, so lower points get more rain and heavier snow. The models' grids are 2 to 3 km wide at best, so nearby points often share the same precipitation and differ mainly through temperature. The rest of the page uses Mid-Collins only.

  • GFS + HRRR line: the snow from the three steps above.
  • NBM and NAM lines: Open-Meteo reports their snowfall at a fixed 7 to 1 ratio, so the page converts it back to snow water and applies the same Kuchera ratio. That way the lines differ only in how much precipitation each model expects.

other model's snow = (its snowfall ÷ 7) × Kuchera ratio

  • Average: for each hour, the mean of the models that still have a forecast, then added up. NAM ends after about 2.5 days and NBM after about 11, so beyond that the average is the GFS alone.
  • Labels on the thick lines: where an average stays flat (changing less than 0.1") for a long stretch, the chart prints the location, the total so far and, in brackets, the matching snow water.

Cloud cover by model

Each thin line is one model's cloud cover, from 0% (clear) to 100% (overcast). The models share one colour and are told apart by line pattern, the same patterns as on the snowfall chart, with ECMWF AI drawn dash-dot. The key under the chart shows which is which. The thick line is the hour-by-hour average, drawn while at least two models have data. It changes colour with its own value, from bright blue at 0% cloud to dark grey at 100%, so blue stretches are the clear-sky windows. No calculation is applied; these are the models' own numbers. Read it with the snowfall chart to find clearing after a storm.

The background is shaded pale yellow while the sun is up and slightly darker at night, using the forecast service's hourly day or night flag for Mid-Collins. The edges are accurate to about half an hour, and they mark sunrise and sunset on a flat horizon, not when the sun clears the ridges.

Wind rose

There are two, one for Mid-Collins and one for the top of Mt Baldy. Each shows where the wind comes from over the selected period. A wedge points to a compass direction; its length is the share of hours the wind came from there, and the colour bands split that into wind speeds. Each uses the GFS wind 10 metres above the ground at that point.

The two roses often look alike. The points are about a kilometre apart, which is less than one model grid square, and the model does not capture how much stronger the wind is on an exposed summit.

wedge length = hours from that direction ÷ total hours × 100

Wind loading potential

There are two, one for Mid-Collins and one for the top of Mt Baldy, each using that point's own snowfall and wind.

Estimates which slope aspects the wind is loading with snow. Each wedge points at the aspect being loaded, which is the opposite side from where the wind comes from: a south wind loads north-facing slopes. A longer, darker wedge means more loading.

For every hour, the page takes the snow that fell and counts a share of it as drifted, depending on wind speed:

drift share = (wind speed − 10) ÷ 20, kept between 0 and 1
wind-drifted snow = snowfall × drift share

So nothing drifts below 10 mph, half of the snow counts at 20 mph, and all of it at 30 mph or more. Each hour's drifted snow is added to the aspect the wind was blowing toward.

  • Inputs: the model-average snowfall and the GFS wind 10 metres above the ground at this location.
  • Levels: Low under 1", Moderate 1–3", High 3–6", Very high 6" or more of drifted snow over the selected period.
  • Limits: it ignores terrain shape, stronger wind at ridgetops, and wind moving snow already on the ground. The speed limits and levels are rules of thumb chosen for this page.

This is not an avalanche forecast. Check the Utah Avalanche Center before going into avalanche terrain.

Temperature

The GFS air temperature 2 metres above the ground at the Base, Mid-Collins and Top of Collins, each adjusted by Open-Meteo to that point's elevation. Every line is red above freezing and blue at or below it, and the thin grey line marks 32°F. The locations are told apart by line style: Mid-Collins is thick and solid, the Base is dashed and Top of Collins is dotted.

Wind

The GFS wind speed and gusts 10 metres above the ground. The small arrows point where the wind is blowing toward, so an arrow pointing up means a south wind. No calculation is applied.

Rain & water

Hourly amounts from the GFS. "Rain" is the model's rain plus showers. "Water" is all precipitation measured as liquid, including the water in snow, so water minus rain is the snow water used in the snow calculation.

Snow density

Shown only for hours when it snows. Density is 100 ÷ the Kuchera ratio. Lower numbers mean lighter snow: under 7% is light powder, 7–11% is average, and above 11% is heavy.

16-day summary

One row per day, from the GFS hourly forecast.

  • Hi / Lo, Wind / Gust: the highest and lowest temperature, and the strongest wind and gust, of the day.
  • Dir: the day's main wind direction, an average that gives stronger winds more weight.
  • Clouds: the average cloud cover.
  • Water, Snow, Rain: the day's snow water, snow and rain added up.
  • Density: the day's snow water ÷ snow × 100.
  • NWS snow: the National Weather Service's own snowfall forecast for this point, added up by day and converted from millimetres to inches. It covers about a week and is independent of the calculations above.

National Weather Service forecast

The official forecast text for this point from the Salt Lake City office, shown as issued. Hover over a period to read the full wording.
