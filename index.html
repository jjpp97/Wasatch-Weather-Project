Where the data comes from

Everything is a model forecast for one point: Mid-Collins at Alta, 9,662 ft. The exceptions are the snowfall chart, which also shows three other points at Alta, and the temperature chart, which adds Base and Top of Collins. Each resort has its own page, picked with the resort tabs at the top of the page; everything described here works the same way on each. Apart from the Weather Stations tab and the measured snow boxes at the top of the Forecast tab, nothing here is a measurement, including the "Now" tiles.

How often it updates. Weather data is downloaded at most once every three minutes. If you reload the page, reopen it or switch resort within three minutes of the last download, the page reuses the copy your browser saved instead of asking the weather services again. "Updated" at the top right gives the time the forecast was actually downloaded.

  • Main forecast: the US GFS model from Open-Meteo, with the high-resolution HRRR model filling in the first one to two days. It runs 16 days ahead in hourly steps.
  • What is averaged and what is not: snowfall, cloud cover, temperature and wind speed are the hour-by-hour average of the models that have data for that hour, so a single model running hot, cold or windy counts for less. For temperature and wind speed an hour needs at least two models; otherwise the GFS value is used, which only happens in the last day or so of the 16. NAM ends after about 2.5 days and NBM after about 11, so the mix of models behind the average changes with range. Gusts, wind direction, "feels like", rain amounts, the snow-to-water ratio, the snow level and everything "already fallen" still come from the GFS + HRRR alone, either because the other models are not downloaded for them or because they are worked out from several GFS values that have to agree with each other. If the wind average comes out above the GFS gust for an hour, the gust is raised to match it.
  • Comparison models: the NBM (the National Weather Service's blend of many models, about 11 days) and the NAM (about 2.5 days). The ECMWF AI model appears on the cloud chart only.
  • National Weather Service: the text forecast at the bottom of the Forecast tab.

The page has four tabs under the location name. Forecast holds the summary charts, daily forecast and tiles. Backcountry Ski Metrics is in development and empty for now. Spaghettis holds the charts that compare the individual models, snowfall and cloud cover. Weather Stations holds measured readings.

The four snow tiles on the Forecast tab are buttons. Click one to make the time-based charts show only that period: 24 hours, 72 hours, 7 days or 16 days. The Spaghettis tab has the same choice as a row of buttons, and the two stay in step.

Every chart with a time axis starts at midnight today, and on the 16-day view they all end on the same day, with their scales in a column of the same width on the right. That puts each day at the same place across the page on the Snow Summary, the Daily Forecast buttons, and the temperature, SWE, wind, ratio and model charts. Today's hours before now are dimmed, with a dashed "Now" line: they are the model's record of the day so far, not measurements. Snow totals and the snow tiles still count from now.

Weather Stations

Storm Results, at the top of the Locations menu (the button with three lines), shows every resort's measured snow box on one page, ordered from the most measured storm snow to the least. Each box works the same way as the one on that resort's Forecast tab. The one difference is how the storm's days are picked: Storm Results uses the GFS + HRRR model alone for that, so a storm's last day can occasionally differ by a day from the Snow Summary, which uses the model average.

The only measured data on the page. It shows the latest reading from each station that is reporting, temperature, wind, gust, and the direction the wind is coming from. Alta's and Deer Valley's stations are read live from the National Weather Service's public data feed. Solitude's stations are not on that feed, so a scheduled job collects them from the Synoptic Data service once an hour and saves them with the site; a Solitude reading can therefore be up to an hour or so old, and each station's history only builds up from the day the job started (ten days are kept). A reading more than three hours old is marked "old". The stations are listed by how many wind or snow measurements they sent in the last 24 hours, most first (a wind sensor stuck on zero all day counts for nothing). Ties go to the station with the most readings of any kind in the last 24 hours, which is the count in the last column, and then to the lower station. The top station is the one opened when the tab loads.

Besides wind and temperature, a station gets a chart for each other thing it reports: humidity, dew point, wind chill, heat index, precipitation in the last hour, pressure, and visibility. These sit just above the station's readings table, and a chart only appears if the station has readings for it. The feed reports in metric units, converted here: millimetres ÷ 25.4 = inches, pascals ÷ 3,386.39 = inches of mercury, metres ÷ 1,609.34 = miles.

When a station has four readings or fewer, its charts say very little, so its readings table is shown first and the charts follow it.

If a station's wind sensor, or its thermometer, has read nothing but zero (or nothing at all) over the last 24 hours, it is probably iced up or broken. The charts for that measurement are moved below the station's readings table and given a red background, with a note at the top saying so. This is checked each time the page loads, so the charts go back to their usual place as soon as the sensor reports real values again.

  • Stations: Alta Base, Alta Guard House, Alta Collins, Top of Collins, Hidden Peak (Snowbird) and Mt Baldy. Any that are not sending data are listed under the table and appear on their own once they report.
  • Model check: the line under the table compares the wind a summit station measured over the last few days with the wind the model gave for the Mt Baldy point over the same hours. It uses Mt Baldy's own station when it reports, otherwise Hidden Peak, a summit about a kilometre away.It uses the Honeycomb Peak station.It uses the DV Empire Peak station when it reports wind, otherwise DV Mount Baldy. The "typical miss" is the average size of the hourly difference.

Click a station in the table to see its recent history below: an hour-by-hour wind bar chart (average speed, gusts and direction arrows), a wind rose, a temperature chart, and then every reading line by line, newest first. The temperature chart and the readings table cover the station's last 500 readings, which is about three and a half days for a station that reports every ten minutes and longer for one that reports less often.

Wind bars. One bar per hour. The blue part is the average of the wind speeds the station reported in that hour. The red part on top reaches up to the strongest gust reported in that hour. The arrow above a bar points the way the wind was blowing, using the hour's readings averaged with the stronger ones counting for more. The buttons above the chart set how far back it and the wind rose look: the last 6, 12, 24, 48 or 72 hours, counted from now.

Wind rose. Drawn in the style of Alta's own wind page. An arrow sits on the rim at each direction the wind came from and points inward, the way the wind blew: an arrow at the bottom, pointing up, is a south wind. Its length is that direction's share of the "miles of wind", and the direction with the most reaches the centre. The coloured rings are only there to judge length by. Directions with under 2% of the total are left out.

miles of wind = the hourly average speeds (mph) added up over the period

An hour at 10 mph is 10 miles of air going past, so 24 hours averaging 10 mph is 240 miles of wind. The line under the rose gives the total, the directions most of it came from, and how many readings were used, and tells you if the station's readings do not reach back as far as the period you picked.

Aspects Most Likely to Be Wind Loaded (off the page for now). Each station used to have a second rose beside its wind rose, showing which slopes the wind had loaded. It has been taken off while it is reworked, and the description is kept here for when it returns. This is the index described under "Wind drifting potential" below, worked out for what has already happened at that station:

  • Wind and temperature: what the station measured, averaged into hours.
  • Snowfall: the model's record for the station's exact location, run through the page's snow calculation. The stations do not report snow, so this part is an estimate.
  • Loose snow already on the ground: built up over the ten days before, using the model's snow and, for the hours before the station's readings begin, the model's wind.

Because it uses measured wind, it is the better guide to what the wind has actually done at that spot. A sheltered station can show little or no drifting when the forecast roses, which use the model's wind, show more.

The stations do not feed into any forecast chart or calculation. They are there so you can compare the forecast with what is happening on the mountain.

How snow is calculated

Models forecast precipitation as liquid water. The page turns that into inches of snow in three steps, hour by hour.

1. Snow water (SWE). The part of the precipitation that falls as snow, still measured as liquid:

snow water = total precipitation − rain − showers

2. Snow ratio (Kuchera method). How many inches of snow one inch of water makes. Colder air makes lighter snow and a higher ratio. The method takes the warmest temperature anywhere between the ground and about 18,000 ft (the surface plus five levels above it), in kelvin:

if warmest temperature is above 271.16 K (28.4°F): ratio = 12 + 2 × (271.16 − warmest)
otherwise: ratio = 12 + (271.16 − warmest)

The ratio is kept between 3 and 30. Examples: a warmest temperature of 30°F gives about 10 to 1, and 20°F gives about 17 to 1.

3. Snow.

snow (inches) = snow water × ratio

So 0.5" of snow water at 10 to 1 is 5" of snow, and the same water at 17 to 1 is 8.5".

About the Kuchera method. It was fitted in 2004 to snow totals as people measure them, and it is deliberately harsh on warm storms to allow for melting on the ground. It has not been published in a scientific journal and can be well off the ratio a storm actually produces, so treat the ratios here as estimates. Keeping the ratio between 3 and 30 is this page's own rule, not part of the method.

Measured snow boxes

The box with a blue left edge, beside Now, Wind and Clouds, holds measurements, not forecasts: the large blue numbers. They come from Alta's Collins snow study plot at 9,662 ft, the same spot as the page's forecast point, through the Synoptic Data service. It reports in whole inches.They come from Solitude's Summit snow study plot at 9,641 ft, the same spot as the page's forecast point, through the Synoptic Data service. It reports in tenths of an inch.They come from Deer Valley's Ontario station at 9,100 ft, the same spot as the page's forecast point, through the Synoptic Data service. It reports in tenths of an inch every five minutes, and the page keeps one reading an hour. Its precipitation gauge barely registers snow, so no SWE is shown for Deer Valley.

  • How new snow is counted: the station has a board that measures the snow that has piled up since staff last cleared it. The page adds snow each time the board rises above its highest reading since it was cleared. A drop of more than 2" is taken to mean the board was cleared; smaller dips are treated as settling or sensor noise and ignored. Readings taken when it is above 40°F are skipped, because the board cannot be gaining snow then and a jump is a sensor glitch. So is a single reading that jumps more than 3" above the readings either side of it when those two agree, which catches the sudden one-off spikes some sensors give.
  • Storm total: the measured snow on the days of the most recent storm shown on the Snow Summary: the one under way, or otherwise the last one that ended. The storm's days come from the forecast and the model record, and the snow in them is measured. For a storm that has not finished, it is the total up to now.
  • Last 12 hours, 24 hours and 7 days: the measured snow in that time, counted back from now.
  • The small "SWE" number beside each (Alta only): the snow water measured by the station's precipitation gauge over the same period. The gauge records the water that fell in each hour but cannot tell snow from rain, so the page only counts hours when it was 35°F or colder at the station. Gauges catch less than really falls when it is windy, so the true figure is often a little higher. Solitude's Summit station has no gauge, and Deer Valley's Ontario gauge barely registers snow, so no SWE is shown at either.
  • The small "model" number under each: the model's own estimate of the snow that fell at the forecast point over the same period. It is the same record that draws the grey "already fallen" bars on the Snow Summary, added up over the storm's days so far or over the last 12 hours, 24 hours or 7 days. Comparing the two shows how well the model has been tracking what really fell.
  • How fresh it is: a scheduled job collects the station's readings once an hour and saves them to a file the page reads. The line under the boxes gives the time of the latest reading and warns if it is more than three hours old.
  • Limits: the station reports whole inches once an hour, so small amounts can be missed. It is one sheltered spot and does not speak for the base or the ridges. Readings from before the job started cannot be recovered, so for a storm that began earlier the line under the box says the record starts partway through the storm.

Snow-to-water ratio

The page describes how light or heavy snow is with a snow-to-water ratio: how many inches of snow you get from one inch of water. "12 to 1" means 12 inches of snow for every inch of water. A higher ratio is lighter, fluffier snow.

  • 14 to 1 or more: light powder (pale blue).
  • 9 to 14 to 1: average snow (mid blue).
  • Under 9 to 1: heavy, wet snow (deep blue).

For a single hour the ratio is the Kuchera ratio from step 2 above. Every model's snow uses that same hourly ratio, so the model average has it too.

For a longer period, such as a day or the day and night parts of a bar, the ratio is the total snow divided by the total snow water:

ratio for a period = total inches of snow ÷ total inches of snow water

That is not the same as averaging the hourly ratios. Dividing the totals gives more weight to the hours when more snow fell, which is what you would measure on the ground. For example, 1" of water at 8 to 1 followed by 0.1" of water at 20 to 1 is 10" of snow from 1.1" of water, or about 9 to 1, not the 14 to 1 that averaging the two ratios would give.

To turn a ratio into the older "percent water" figure, divide 100 by it: 10 to 1 is 10% and 20 to 1 is 5%.

Snow tiles

The "Next 24 h", "Next 72 h", "7-day" and "16-day" tiles show the snow and snow water added up over that period, using the average of the snowfall models at Mid-Collins (see the next chart). The "Now" and "Clouds" tiles use the average of the models for the current hour, the same way as the Daily Forecast: the weather type is worked out from the model-average snow and cloud cover and the GFS rain for this hour (heavy snow is 0.5" an hour or more, snow 0.15", light snow 0.03", rain 0.01" of water), and otherwise from the cloud cover. A single model can show a one-hour gap in the middle of a storm, which the average smooths over. The temperature and the wind speed in the "Wind" tile are the average of the models (GFS + HRRR, NBM, NAM and ECMWF AI) for the current hour; the gust and direction are the GFS model's current values.

Snow Summary

A day-by-day view of snow at Mid-Collins: the last 120 days, today, and the next fifteen days. The chart opens with today at the left edge; scroll it left to go back in time. The number above each bar is that day's total in inches. Above the bars, each storm has a bracket spanning its days, with the storm's total snow written over it.

  • Blue bars (forecast): the average of the snowfall models, the same figure as the snow tiles. The shade of blue shows the snow-to-water ratio, on the same scale as the Snow-to-Water Ratio chart: pale blue is light snow at 14 to 1 or more, mid blue is average snow from 9 to 14 to 1, and deep blue is heavy snow under 9 to 1.
  • Day and night: each forecast bar is split by a thin line into snow falling while the sun is up (the lower part) and snow falling at night (the upper part), both within that calendar day. The night part carries a faint crescent moon when it is tall enough to fit one. The two parts can be different shades, because the ratio is worked out for each separately.
  • Grey bars (already fallen): the GFS model's estimate of what fell, run through the same snow calculation. It is not a measurement, because none of the weather stations the page can reach report snowfall. Today's bar has a grey part for the hours already gone and a blue part for the rest of the day.
  • Where the past comes from: Open-Meteo's archive of past forecasts, downloaded each time the page loads. Nothing is stored by this site, so every visitor sees the same record, and days older than 120 days drop off the left end.
  • Storms under way: for a storm that has started but not finished, the large number is only the snow still to come. The line under it gives how much has already fallen. Add the two for the storm's full total. The SWE chart works the same way.
  • Green totals on the SWE chart: a storm's water total is written in green, the same green as the rain bars, when more than half of that water is forecast (or recorded) as rain. Otherwise it is blue, or grey for a storm that has finished.
  • The small darker blue number beside each storm total: the storm's snow water (SWE) in inches, from the same models as the snow. It covers the same thing as the large number next to it: the whole storm, or for a storm under way only what is still to come. Under a very short bracket the word "SWE" is left off to save room.
  • Rain bars: each day's rain, in inches of water, is stacked in green on top of that day's snow (a darker green for rain that has already fallen). The white number above a bar is still the day's snow only; when the day has rain, its amount is written in green just under that. The scale on the right is inches for both, so remember that an inch of rain is far more water than an inch of snow. Storms are still picked out by their snow, so a day with only rain does not start or extend a storm.
  • The small green number after it: the rain in that storm, in inches of water, shown only when the storm has any. It uses the same rain figures as the SWE chart, and like the other two numbers it covers only what is still to come for a storm under way. Snow water plus rain is the storm's total water.
  • Storm totals: a storm is a run of back-to-back days with at least 0.1" of snow each. One day with less than that ends the storm, so two bursts of snow separated by a dry day count as two storms. The total adds up everything in those bars, including the grey "already fallen" part if the storm has already started.

This chart always shows the full range; the 24 h, 72 h, 7-day and 16-day tiles do not zoom it.

Daily Forecast

A strip with one button per day for Mid-Collins, and under it the chosen day hour by hour.

  • Each day shows: the day and date, a weather symbol, the high and low temperature (red above freezing, blue at or below), and a wind symbol if it is a windy day.
  • Weather symbol: worked out from the page's own numbers so it agrees with the charts. Heavy snow is 6" or more of model-average snow, snow is 0.3" or more, rain is 0.1" or more of rain, and otherwise the sky is sunny, partly cloudy or cloudy from the average daytime cloud cover (under 25%, under 60%, or more).
  • Wind symbol: shown when the day's strongest wind reaches 15 mph. Its colour follows the wind roses: light orange under 20 mph, orange for 20–30 mph and red for 30 mph or more.
  • Hourly chart: click a day to see its hours, and use the buttons under the days to change what is shown. Today shows all 24 hours: the hours that have already passed are dimmed, with a dashed "Now" line where the forecast takes over. The dimmed hours are the model's record of the day so far, not measurements.

The measures:

  • Precip chance: the chance of any precipitation in that hour, averaged over the models that publish one (GFS and NBM). The bar colour says what would fall, using the same model-average snow water and GFS rain as the SWE chart: pale blue for snow, green for rain, grey where there is a chance but no amount forecast.
  • Snow density: the snow-to-water ratio for each hour that has snow, in inches of snow per inch of water, so a taller bar is lighter snow. The colours are the same three bands as the Snow Summary. Hours with no snow have no bar.
  • Cloud cover: the model average from the Cloud cover by model chart. Each bar is bluer the clearer the sky and greyer the cloudier, over yellow day and dark night bands.
  • Temperature and Feels like: the temperature is the average of the models (GFS + HRRR, NBM, NAM and ECMWF AI); "Feels like" is still the GFS model's own figure. Both are drawn with red bars above freezing, blue bars at or below, and a dashed line at 32°F. The bars rise from the bottom of the chart, not from zero. "Feels like" accounts for wind and humidity.
  • Wind & gusts: the model-average wind as a bar in the wind rose colours, with the gust rising above it in faint pink at the same width. The label above gives the wind with the gust in brackets, such as "17 (28)". Above that is the direction for that hour: the compass point the wind comes from, with a small arrow showing where it is blowing toward.

Every measure is drawn as bars with the value written above each one. On a narrow screen only every second or third bar is labelled.

Snowfall by model

Running totals of snow from now, with one chart for each forecast point except the ridge-top one (Mt Baldy), lowest first. On each chart the thin coloured lines are the individual models (GFS + HRRR orange, NBM yellow, NAM blue) and the thick white line is their average. Where the thin lines sit close together the models agree; where they fan out the forecast is uncertain. Click a model in the legend above a chart to hide or show it.

Each point is run through the same snow calculation with its own temperatures, so lower points get more rain and heavier snow. The models' grids are 2 to 3 km wide at best, so nearby points often share the same precipitation and differ mainly through temperature. The rest of the page uses Mid-Collins only.

  • GFS + HRRR line: the snow from the three steps above.
  • NBM and NAM lines: Open-Meteo reports their snowfall at a fixed 7 to 1 ratio, so the page converts it back to snow water and applies the same Kuchera ratio. That way the lines differ only in how much precipitation each model expects.
  • ECMWF IFS line (pink): the European centre's main model at 9 km, drawn for comparison only. It is not in the average, so it does not change the white line, the snow boxes or the Snow Summary. Its snowfall is converted the same way as NAM's.

other model's snow = (its snowfall ÷ 7) × Kuchera ratio

  • Average: for each hour, the mean of the models that still have a forecast, then added up. NAM ends after about 2.5 days and NBM after about 11, so beyond that the average is the GFS alone.
  • Labels on the thick lines: where an average stays flat (changing less than 0.1") for a long stretch, the chart prints "Average", the total so far and, in brackets, the matching snow water.

Cloud cover by model

Each thin line is one model's cloud cover, from 0% (clear) to 100% (overcast). The models share one colour and are told apart by line pattern, the same patterns as on the snowfall chart, with ECMWF AI drawn dash-dot. The key under the chart shows which is which. The thick line is the hour-by-hour average, drawn while at least two models have data. It changes colour with its own value, from bright blue at 0% cloud to dark grey at 100%, so blue stretches are the clear-sky windows. No calculation is applied; these are the models' own numbers. Read it with the snowfall chart to find clearing after a storm.

The background is shaded pale yellow while the sun is up and slightly darker at night, using the forecast service's hourly day or night flag for Mid-Collins. The edges are accurate to about half an hour, and they mark sunrise and sunset on a flat horizon, not when the sun clears the ridges.

Temperature by model and Wind by model

On the Spaghettis tab. Each thin line is one model's own forecast for Mid-Collins: the air temperature 2 metres above the ground, or the wind speed 10 metres above it. The models are told apart by line pattern, as on the cloud chart, and the thick line is the hour-by-hour average of the models that have data, drawn while at least two do. No calculation is applied beyond that average.

  • Models: GFS + HRRR, NBM, NAM and ECMWF AI. NAM ends after about 2.5 days and NBM after about 11.
  • Temperature: the average is red above freezing and blue at or below, with a thin line at 32°F.
  • Wind: the models can differ a lot at one point in the mountains. The GFS + HRRR line often runs well above the others at Mid-Collins.

These charts are for comparing the models. The thick average line for temperature and for wind speed is what the Forecast tab uses: its Temperature and Wind charts, the temperature and wind in the Daily Forecast, and the Now and Wind tiles.

Wind drifting potential

None of the wind drifting roses are on the page at the moment; this section describes how they work for when they return. Each station on the Weather Stations tab had a wind drifting rose beside its wind rose. It shows which slope aspects the wind loaded with drifted snow over the period picked. The forecast versions for Mid-Collins and Mt Baldy, which use the model's wind, are off the page while the Backcountry Ski Metrics tab is in development. A wedge points at the aspect being loaded, which is the opposite side from where the wind comes from: a south wind loads north-facing slopes. A longer, darker wedge means more drifting.

The number is an index, not an amount of snow. For every hour it multiplies two things, each between 0 and 1:

drifting potential = wind strength × snow supply

Wind strength. Below a threshold speed the wind moves nothing. Above it, the amount of snow the wind can carry rises very steeply, with about the fourth power of the wind speed, reaching full strength at 30 mph:

wind strength = (wind⁴ − threshold⁴) ÷ (30⁴ − threshold⁴), kept between 0 and 1

The threshold depends on the state of the snow:

  • 10 mph in an hour when it is snowing, because falling and fresh snow moves easily.
  • 17 mph for dry snow that has settled.
  • 22 mph when it is above freezing, because wet snow sticks.

The fourth power makes a big difference: with settled snow, a 25 mph wind has about three times the strength of a 20 mph wind.

Snow supply. Snow can drift for days after it falls, so the page keeps a running pool of loose snow, in inches. Each hour:

  • New snow is added to the pool.
  • The pool ages. Snow bonds together as it sits, so it gets steadily harder to move. At or below freezing the pool halves every 36 hours. Above freezing it halves every 3 hours.
  • Wind uses it up. A full-strength wind removes a quarter of the pool each hour, since that snow has been moved and packed.

snow supply = loose snow ÷ (loose snow + 2 inches)

So 2" of loose snow gives a supply of one half and 6" gives three quarters. The pool is started ten days before now, from the model's record, so that snow already on the ground counts.

The wedges. Each hour's potential is added to the aspect the wind was blowing toward. The total for an aspect reads as "hours of full-strength drifting": Low is under 0.5, Moderate 0.5–2, High 2–6 and Very high 6 or more over the selected period. The steps are small because moderate winds score well under 1 an hour. With plenty of loose snow, about 12 hours of 20 mph wind lands in Moderate, 12 hours of 25 mph in High and 10 hours of 30 mph in Very high. Hovering a wedge shows the level, how many hours had drifting, the typical wind in those hours and how much loose snow was available.

Where the numbers come from.

  • From research: the thresholds (about 10 mph for fresh snow, 17 mph for dry and 22 mph for wet snow, from Li and Pomeroy and from Liston), the steep rise with wind speed (measured at about the fourth power on open ground by Tabler, and the third power for deposition behind a ridge by Föhn), and the fact that snow gets harder to move with time and warmth (Schmidt; Li and Pomeroy).
  • Chosen for this page: the rates at which the loose snow ages and is used up, the 2" supply figure, the 30 mph full-strength speed and the four levels. The research shows these effects exist but gives no agreed rates, so treat the index as a way to compare aspects and days, not as a measurement.
  • Inputs: the model-average snowfall and the GFS wind and temperature 10 metres above the ground at each point. The ten-day run-up uses the Mid-Collins record for both roses.
  • Not included: terrain shape, stronger wind on ridgetops, sun crusts and rain crusts.

This is not an avalanche forecast. Check the Utah Avalanche Center before going into avalanche terrain.

SWE

SWE stands for snow water equivalent: the depth of water you would get by melting the snow. This chart is the Snow Summary's layout, but for liquid: each day's bar stacks the rain and the snow water (SWE) that fell or is forecast, in inches of water. Together they are all of the day's precipitation. Storms are grouped and totalled above the bars in the same way, and the chart scrolls left through the last 120 days.

  • Rain: the GFS model's rain plus showers.
  • Snow water (SWE): for the forecast, the average of the snowfall models, the same figure as the "water" on the snow tiles. For past days, the GFS model's estimate.
  • Storms here include rain, so they can start earlier or run longer than the same storm on the Snow Summary. A day counts once it has at least 0.01" of water.

Like the Snow Summary, this chart always shows the full range and is not zoomed by the tiles.

Snow Level chart

The snow level is the height above which precipitation falls as snow and below which it falls as rain. The thick pale blue line is the estimated snow level hour by hour, and the dashed red line above it is the freezing level, the height where the air is at 32°F. The thin flat lines mark the heights of this resort's forecast points (Base (8,560 ft), Mid-Collins (9,662 ft), Top of Collins (10,443 ft) and Mt Baldy (11,068 ft)), each in its usual colour. A point whose line sits above the blue line should get snow; one below it should get rain.

snow level = freezing level − 1,000 ft

  • Freezing level: from the GFS + HRRR model, as a height above sea level.
  • Why 1,000 ft: snowflakes do not melt the moment they fall into air above freezing. They usually survive roughly 1,000 ft below the freezing level, which is the rule of thumb forecasters use. The real gap varies from a few hundred feet in light precipitation and humid air to 1,500 ft or more in heavy precipitation or very dry air, so treat the line as accurate to a few hundred feet at best.
  • Blue, green and dim: the line is dim when the model is dry, since the snow level only matters when something is falling. When precipitation is forecast it is pale blue if it would fall as snow at Mid-Collins and green if it would fall as rain there, the same green the other charts use for rain. An hour counts as rain when the model has more rain than snow water at that point.
  • Hovering shows both heights, whether precipitation is forecast, and which forecast points are above the snow level.
